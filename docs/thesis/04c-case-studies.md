# Chapter 4 — Single-DApp Case Studies with Triangulation

## Overview

The preceding sections characterise the DApp landscape at the cross-sectional and ecosystem level. This section descends to the individual protocol level, using a three-layer triangulation structure to verify whether single DApps confirm, contradict, or refine aggregate patterns:

1. **Layer 1 — Market-wide evidence**: what statistical pattern exists across all 855 DApps?
2. **Layer 2 — Ecosystem evidence**: where is that pattern strongest or most anomalous in the relevant sector?
3. **Layer 3 — Single-DApp case study**: how and why does the specific protocol instantiate, amplify, or contradict the pattern?

Five cases were selected to maximise coverage across the governance spectrum and revenue typology identified in §4.6: Uniswap (DeFi / DEX), Aave (DeFi / lending), Polymarket (prediction markets), Virtuals Protocol (AI-native infrastructure), and Ethena (RWA / synthetic dollar). Together they span the full governance range from team-controlled to on-chain DAO, and the full business-model range from transaction fees to interest margins and take-rate platform economics. Cross-references to §4.6 and the anomaly register in §4.9 are embedded throughout.

## §4.11 Case Study 1 — Uniswap: Governance at Scale in a Competitive DEX Market

### Layer 1: Market-Wide Evidence

The market-wide picture relevant to Uniswap's case combines two findings from §4.3: market concentration is extreme (top-10 DApps hold 80.46 per cent of strict-sample market cap, INS-MKT-01) and governance quality co-varies positively with scale (Spearman r=0.38, §4.3.4). Fully decentralised DApps are disproportionately represented among market-cap leaders and exclusively use on-chain governance mechanisms (§4.2.2, Table 4.2). Multi-chain deployment carries a 1.3× market cap premium, and 70.59 per cent of the strict sample is multi-chain (§4.4.2). The cross-sectional design cannot establish causation, but the pattern poses a clear question: does governance leadership coexist with sustained competitive scale in a high-competition DEX market?

### Layer 2: Ecosystem Evidence (DeFi / DEX)

Within the DeFi sector (N=105 loose, N=36 strict), the decentralisation rate is 13.3 per cent (§4.6.1) — nearly three times the full-loose-universe average of 4.7 per cent — and all fully decentralised DeFi DApps use on-chain governance mechanisms. Transaction fees are the dominant revenue mechanism: 55.6 per cent of strict-sample DeFi DApps use transaction fees as their primary revenue generator, with swap fees ranging from 0.01 per cent (tight liquidity pools) to 0.30 per cent (exotic pairs). Multi-chain adoption in DeFi reaches 55.2 per cent — the highest of any ecosystem — reflecting aggressive capital-following behaviour as DeFi protocols deploy wherever liquidity density and user activity justify the gas and deployment cost. The registered anomaly ANO-DeFi-03 (§4.6.1) complicates the governance-revenue relationship: three of the five highest-volume DeFi DApps in the dataset are team-controlled or hybrid-governed, demonstrating that revenue generation does not require governance formalisation. This creates the empirical question Uniswap's case helps answer: *does governance leadership coexist with sustained competitive scale?*

### Layer 3: Uniswap — Governance Leadership as Competitive Infrastructure

Uniswap is the canonical example of a DeFi protocol that has traversed the full governance lifecycle: from team-deployed contract to Snapshot off-chain voting to fully on-chain DAO governance. Its governance type is classified as on-chain token governance and its level of decentralisation is Decentralised — one of the nine DApps in the strict sample (13.24 per cent) to achieve this classification.

**Governance mechanics.** Uniswap governance operates through UNI token-weighted voting on Uniswap Improvement Proposals (UIPs). Proposals pass through a two-phase process: a temperature-check Snapshot poll followed by an on-chain vote requiring a 4 per cent quorum of the total UNI supply and a 50 per cent simple majority. Passing proposals are executed through a timelock contract with a 48-hour delay, providing a safety window for liquidity providers and LPs to exit positions before parameter changes take effect. The UNI fee-switch mechanism — allowing token holders to redirect a share of swap fees from liquidity providers to the protocol treasury — was partially activated following a 2024 governance vote, representing a material transfer of economic authority from the team to token holders.

**Business model.** Uniswap's primary revenue mechanism is transaction fees: each swap charges 0.01–0.30 per cent of the traded notional, split between liquidity providers and (following the fee-switch activation) the protocol treasury. The v3 concentrated liquidity model, deployed across Ethereum mainnet and nine additional EVM-compatible chains, allows liquidity providers to specify price ranges, increasing capital efficiency and enabling Uniswap to compete with central-limit order books for price-sensitive traders. The v4 "hooks" architecture, which permits custom pool logic without redeploying the core contracts, extends this model into an application platform rather than a single AMM product.

**Competitive position.** Uniswap occupies a structural duopoly with Raydium (Solana-native) at the top of the DEX market by volume. Its competitive moats are composability — the UniswapV2 and V3 pair interfaces are the canonical integration target for on-chain protocols — and brand trust, which is materially influenced by its governance track record. A key competitive risk is the DEX aggregator layer: protocols such as 1inch, Jumper Exchange, and OpenOcean route liquidity through Uniswap but capture user relationships and a share of the fee spread, effectively reducing Uniswap's pricing power over retail users. Multi-chain deployment on Ethereum, Arbitrum, Base, Optimism, Polygon, Celo, and BNB Chain (§4.4.1) mitigates chain-concentration risk.

**Ecosystem illustration.** Uniswap validates the market-wide pattern: a fully decentralised, on-chain-governed DApp occupies a top-tier market-cap position and operates multi-chain at scale. It also refines ANO-DeFi-03 — governance credibility sustains protocol-level fee revenue and institutional composability even as team-controlled forks (SushiSwap, PancakeSwap) provide continuous competitive pressure. The governance costs — timelock delays, quorum risk, slower parameter changes — appear absorbed by the brand and liquidity premiums that formal decentralisation generates.

## §4.12 Case Study 2 — Aave: Interest Margin Leadership and On-Chain Governance in DeFi Lending

### Layer 1: Market-Wide Evidence

Lending protocols are the second-largest DeFi revenue category by mechanism (interest margin, 16.7 per cent of strict-sample DeFi DApps, §4.6.1) and the primary locus of the TVL leverage anomaly alongside restaking protocols (ANO-MKT-02, §4.9.4). The governance–performance correlation (r=0.38, §4.3.4) is especially salient here: a lending protocol modifiable at will by its team faces adverse selection against institutional capital, making credible governance a direct input to TVL acquisition.

### Layer 2: Ecosystem Evidence (DeFi / Lending)

Within the DeFi lending sub-vertical, on-chain governance is disproportionately prevalent. The interest margin lending category in the strict sample — which includes Aave V3, Morpho, Maple, Moonwell, ZeroLend, and Velo — is the sub-vertical with the highest concentration of on-chain governance mechanisms among DeFi DApps. Governance token issuance reaches 55 per cent of strict-sample DeFi DApps (§4.6.1), and token-holder value accrual is present in approximately 44 per cent of those cases — a lower proportion than governance token issuance, indicating that fee-switch activation lags token issuance across the sector. The registered anomaly ANO-DeFi-01 (§4.6.1) affects several lending protocols: Morpho exhibits a TVL/MCap ratio of approximately 3,437×, and Maple shows extreme capital velocity (volume exceeds TVL by 13.1×, ANO-RWA-02). Aave, by contrast, presents a moderate TVL/MCap relationship, making it the structurally cleanest case study for the lending archetype.

### Layer 3: Aave — Governance Token as Institutional Trust Anchor

Aave V3 is classified with on-chain token governance, a governance token type, and a Decentralised level of decentralisation in the strict sample (Table 4.5). Its governance score is among the highest in the dataset, and it appears in the "Leading" cluster identified by the K-means performance analysis (§4.8): above the 75th percentile on market cap, TVL, and volume, with the highest median governance scores of any performance cluster.

**Governance mechanics.** Aave Governance V3 uses AAVE and stkAAVE token-weighted voting via a two-phase process: a Snapshot community poll followed by on-chain execution through Aave governance executor contracts. Critical proposals use a one- or seven-day timelock depending on assessed risk. The Safety Module (stkAAVE and stkGHO pools) provides a first-loss capital buffer: up to 30 per cent of staked tokens can be slashed to cover a shortfall, aligning staker incentives with protocol health. The Risk Stewards framework (V3.1) allows elected committee members to adjust risk parameters within DAO-approved bounds without a full governance vote, addressing the responsiveness limitation of pure on-chain governance.

**Business model.** Aave earns its revenue through the interest rate spread between borrowers and lenders: the protocol retains a protocol reserve factor (typically 10–20 per cent of the interest margin on each asset) from each lending market. Its primary revenue mechanism is interest margin, with interest yield as the sustainability model. GHO, Aave's native overcollateralised stablecoin launched in 2023, introduces a second revenue stream: GHO minting interest accrues directly to the Aave DAO treasury rather than being distributed to liquidity providers, reducing Aave's cost of capital for the GHO stability module. Token-holder value capture is achieved through GHO interest accruals and the safety module's staked AAVE reward flows.

**Competitive position.** Aave occupies the leading position in non-infrastructure DeFi lending by TVL, competing primarily with Compound (a governance peer with lower TVL), Morpho (a modular layer on top of Aave and Compound that exhibits the extreme TVL/MCap inversion documented as ANO-DeFi-01), and Spark Protocol (a MakerDAO/Sky incubated lending fork). Its multi-chain deployment — Ethereum mainnet, Arbitrum, Polygon, Avalanche, Optimism, Base, BNB Chain, Gnosis, Scroll, and ZkSync — places it among the most broadly deployed DeFi protocols in the dataset (§4.4.2). The Risk Stewards governance innovation represents a competitive differentiator in institutional DeFi: by enabling faster risk parameter updates within DAO-approved bounds, Aave can onboard new collateral assets and adjust liquidation thresholds faster than pure on-chain governance would allow.

**Ecosystem illustration.** Aave confirms that the governance-scale correlation (r=0.38, §4.3.4) holds in lending: the most formally governed protocol commands the largest TVL, because institutional depositors price governance credibility into their allocation decisions. It partially contradicts ANO-DeFi-03: while team-controlled launchpads generate higher raw transaction volumes, Aave's interest margin model sustains deep TVL across market cycles without speculative token emission. The tension with Morpho's extreme TVL/MCap inversion (ANO-DeFi-01) illustrates that TVL leadership does not track governance architecture monotonically — an infrastructure layer can capture more TVL than a governed peer through recursive collateral architecture alone.

## §4.13 Case Study 3 — Polymarket: Team Control as a Structural Requirement in Prediction Markets

### Layer 1: Market-Wide Evidence

The market-wide finding that 86.8 per cent of strict-sample DApps are not fully decentralised (§4.2.1) conceals very different reasons for centralisation across sectors. ANO-GOV-01 (§4.9.1) documents one failure mode — governance tokens without decision-making authority. Polymarket is structurally distinct: team control without any governance token, in a protocol whose business model actively precludes the standard decentralisation trajectory.

### Layer 2: Ecosystem Evidence (Prediction Markets)

Prediction markets are the most centralised of the five ecosystems examined in §4.6: 71.0 per cent team-controlled, 100 per cent classified as Centralised (71.0 per cent) or Semi-Decentralised (29.0 per cent), and zero DApps achieving the Decentralised classification (§4.6.2, Table 4.9). Polymarket alone accounts for 66.6 per cent of sector users and 95.3 per cent of sector volume ($858.8 million of $900.7 million total), creating a market structure where one team-controlled protocol dominates the information-aggregation function for the entire sector. The near-total absence of governance tokens (74.2 per cent tokenless, §4.6.2) is commercially rational: a native token in an information-aggregation platform introduces speculative dynamics that pollute the price signal the platform is designed to generate. The sector-level anomaly ANO-PRED-01 (§4.6.2) documents Polymarket's volume-per-user ratio of $3,992 — placing it among high-volume-per-user DeFi protocols despite serving a retail prediction function, which reflects large-position wagering on major political and macroeconomic events rather than broad-based retail participation.

### Layer 3: Polymarket — Centralisation as Operational Architecture

Polymarket is classified as team-controlled, company-owned, and Centralised, and operates without a native token (Table 4.8). It is the only prediction market DApp to exceed 100,000 active users in the dataset and operates exclusively on Polygon, where it migrated from Ethereum in 2020 to reduce per-resolution transaction costs.

**Governance mechanics.** Polymarket has no formal governance mechanism — no token vote, no Snapshot poll, no multisig community input. Market creation, resolution criteria, oracle selection, and fee parameters are all set by the Polymarket team. Outcome resolution routes through UMA's optimistic oracle: an asserter proposes an outcome, and a 48-hour dispute window allows any party to challenge the result by staking UMA tokens. If challenged, UMA tokenholders arbitrate. In practice, this means outcome resolution authority rests with the Polymarket team (for standard resolutions) and UMA's decentralised oracle (for disputes). The team retains absolute authority over market creation, platform access, geographic restrictions (enforced via front-end geo-fencing for US users following CFTC guidance), and fee rates. This architecture makes Polymarket operationally faster than any governance-mediated prediction market: new markets for breaking events can be launched within hours, and resolution disputes are resolved within the UMA framework without requiring a token-holder vote.

**Business model.** Polymarket charges approximately 2 per cent on winning positions at resolution, implying ~$17.2 million in annualised fee revenue at $858.8 million period volume (§4.6.2). The tokenless model eliminates liquidity mining costs and avoids the regulatory exposure a native prediction-market token would generate under securities or commodities law. Its 2023 Series B ($45 million, led by Founders Fund) is documented in the DeFiLlama raises database; the ROI calculation in §4.9.2 applies to this investment given the absence of a public market valuation.

**Competitive position.** The market structure of the prediction market sector is winner-take-most, driven by liquidity network effects: a market with the deepest liquidity provides the most accurate price signal, attracting more participants and deepening liquidity further. Polymarket's 95.3 per cent volume share reflects this dynamic in full. Its primary competitors — Overtime Markets, PRDT Finance, Limitless — collectively account for less than 5 per cent of sector volume. The Polymarket team's ability to rapidly create markets on high-attention events (US elections, sports tournaments, economic data releases) is a direct function of its centralised architecture; a DAO-governed equivalent would require proposal and voting periods that would delay market availability past the point of peak interest.

**Ecosystem illustration.** Polymarket poses the sharpest challenge to the DApp narrative that equates centralisation with fragility. In prediction markets, centralisation is not a transitional state — it is the equilibrium architecture. The structural reasons are threefold (§4.6.2): outcome resolution requires rapid authoritative intervention; token issuance would distort the information-aggregation function; and regulatory compliance requires a corporate entity with identifiable legal accountability. This case illustrates the insight codified in §4.9.1: governance mechanisms must be evaluated against the operational requirements of the protocol's value proposition, not against a universal governance ladder.

## §4.14 Case Study 4 — Virtuals Protocol: The AI Governance Paradox

### Layer 1: Market-Wide Evidence

Two market-wide findings frame this case (both detailed in §4.6.3): zero AI DApps achieve the Decentralised classification (INS-AI-01), establishing a sector-level structural ceiling; and the AI sector's MCap-to-user inversion (ANO-AI-02) shows that infrastructure protocols are priced on option value rather than current usage — analogous to the TVL leverage phenomenon in DeFi (ANO-MKT-02, §4.9.4).

### Layer 2: Ecosystem Evidence (AI-Native Infrastructure)

Among the three AI infrastructure archetypes identified in §4.6.3, the "AI infrastructure and agent platforms" sub-type — approximately 7–10 DApps in the loose universe — commands disproportionately high market capitalisation relative to user counts. Virtuals Protocol carries $606.3 million in MCap with 39,464 active users (MCap-per-user ≈ $15,365), compared with Hot Spring — The Cozy World which holds 2.9 million users and zero MCap (§4.6.3, Table 4.12). The AI sector's strong Snapshot off-chain governance representation (39.4 per cent versus 17.5 per cent for non-AI DApps, Table in §4.6.3) reflects a pattern of early governance signalling without binding on-chain authority — a configuration that the research comments for FishWar, ZoRobotics, and ChainOpera AI describe as "team likely executes" and "practical control remains with project team today." Virtuals Protocol stands out as the single AI DApp with on-chain token governance — and simultaneously illustrates why this formal apparatus does not resolve the structural centralisation problem identified in INS-AI-01.

### Layer 3: Virtuals Protocol — On-Chain Governance Without Operational Decentralisation

Virtuals Protocol is classified with on-chain token governance, company ownership, and a Centralised decentralisation level, with a governance token type (veVIRTUAL staking). It is deployed on Base and represents the "AI infrastructure and agent platforms" archetype: a permissioned launchpad on which AI agent tokens are deployed through a bonding-curve mechanism.

**Governance mechanics.** Virtuals Protocol governance operates through the veVIRTUAL staking model: VIRTUAL token holders lock tokens to receive veVIRTUAL, which grants voting rights and a share of protocol fee distributions. An active DAO forum processes proposals for protocol parameter changes (fee rates, treasury allocations, agent-launch criteria). On-chain execution routes through a timelocked governance contract. The research annotation for Virtuals Protocol reads: "eVIRTUAL tokenholders vote on-chain; DAO/forum active; team still builds products but governance powers sit with veVIRTUAL holders → high decentralization." This is the most formally governed AI DApp in the dataset.

Yet Virtuals Protocol is classified as Centralised. The distinction — "the Virtuals Protocol paradox" formalised in §4.6.3 — arises from the gap between governance architecture and operational control: token holders vote on protocol parameters but do not govern the AI agent curation or model infrastructure that constitutes the core product. This instantiates INS-AI-01: because AI inference (off-chain model weights, centralised endpoints) cannot be placed under on-chain governance with current technology, no DApp whose primary value is AI-agent functionality can achieve effective operational decentralisation regardless of its financial governance structure.

**Business model.** Virtuals Protocol earns a take-rate on the bonding-curve liquidity committed when new AI agents are launched on the platform. This model is analogous to memecoin or token launchpad economics (comparable to Pump.fun's model in the DeFi sector) but applied to autonomous AI agents: every new agent launch generates protocol revenue proportional to the initial liquidity pool size. The $7.8 million in recorded volume (§4.6.3, Table 4.12) represents a snapshot of launch activity; the MCap of $606.3 million reflects market pricing of the long-run take-rate revenue stream if AI agent proliferation reaches the scale implied by the broader AI agent narrative. The veVIRTUAL fee-distribution mechanism channels a share of take-rate revenue to governance participants, partially realising the governance-token-as-revenue-claim model.

**Competitive position.** Virtuals Protocol competes with Pump.fun (team-controlled, memecoin launchpad), AI16z's Eliza OS framework, and emerging Base-native agent platforms. Its governance token model and on-chain launch mechanics differentiate it from purely team-controlled alternatives: the veVIRTUAL staking creates a committed holder base with financial incentive to advocate for platform growth. The Base chain positioning is strategically significant — Base hosts the highest concentration of venture-backed AI DApps in the dataset (§4.6.3, Table 4.11), providing ecosystem network effects for agent composability. The competitive risk is protocol cloning: the bonding-curve take-rate model is trivially replicable, and the absence of proprietary AI model infrastructure means Virtuals Protocol's moat rests primarily on liquidity aggregation and brand trust rather than technical barriers.

**Ecosystem illustration.** Virtuals Protocol is the AI sector's governance ceiling: the most formally governed AI DApp is still classified Centralised, confirming INS-AI-01 at the individual protocol level. Its MCap-per-user ratio of ~$15,365 with only 39,464 active users directly instantiates ANO-AI-02 — infrastructure protocols are valued as options on the AI-agent economy, not on current usage. For thesis purposes, Virtuals Protocol is the cleanest single-DApp demonstration that governance quality and decentralisation level do not collapse into one dimension for AI-native protocols.

## §4.15 Case Study 5 — Ethena: Synthetic Dollars and the TVL Leverage Anomaly

### Layer 1: Market-Wide Evidence

The TVL leverage anomaly (ANO-MKT-02, §4.9.4) affects 8.82 per cent of the strict sample via three mechanisms: recursive collateral looping (Morpho, EigenLayer), restaking economics (LIDO), and liability-side TVL (Ethena). Ethena is selected here precisely to illustrate the third mechanism, which is the least intuitive and most analytically consequential for practitioners who treat TVL as an unmodified proxy for protocol importance.

### Layer 2: Ecosystem Evidence (RWA / Synthetic Dollar)

The RWA ecosystem (N=43 loose universe, §4.6.4) is characterised by the second-highest centralisation rate among the five ecosystems examined (60.5 per cent team-controlled) and the lowest decentralisation rate (2.3 per cent). Regulatory requirements for corporate custodianship of tokenised real-world assets are the primary structural explanation for this centralisation. Ethena is the exception to the no-token pattern in RWA: while 55.8 per cent of RWA DApps operate without a native token (the highest no-token share of any ecosystem), Ethena issues ENA as both a governance and revenue-sharing instrument. The HYBRID governance coding applied to Ethena (Table 4.14) — the only such coding in the RWA ecosystem — reflects a deliberate architectural choice to combine on-chain token voting for strategic parameters with a Risk Committee empowered to take fast unilateral action on risk management decisions.

### Layer 3: Ethena — Synthetic Dollar Protocol with Hybrid Governance and Liability-Side TVL

Ethena is classified with hybrid governance, company ownership, and a semi-decentralised level, with a utility token type for ENA. The staked ENA (sENA) mechanism enables fee distribution to token holders. Its headline metrics (§4.6.4): 9,104 active users, $892.6 million in period volume, $14,224.9 million TVL, $1,830.8 million MCap — placing the TVL/MCap ratio at approximately 7.8×, registered as ANO-RWA-01.

**Governance mechanics.** Ethena's governance is explicitly designed for the institutional DeFi operational context. ENA tokenholders vote on strategic decisions — new sUSDe yield strategies, RWA collateral additions, protocol fee structures — through an on-chain governance contract. The Risk Committee, however, holds autonomous authority over risk parameter management: it can adjust collateral haircuts, set liquidation thresholds, delist unsafe yield strategies, and pause the protocol without a governance vote, subject to a circuit-breaker that allows ENA tokenholders to override Risk Committee decisions with a supermajority. This hybrid model explicitly trades governance speed for governance legitimacy: it preserves on-chain accountability for strategic decisions while ensuring that a yield strategy that deteriorates overnight can be managed within hours, not weeks.

The hybrid governance coding is substantiated by the research annotation for Ethena: the governance architecture combines "committee-based risk management + token holder voting for strategic decisions." This configuration is analogous to the Risk Stewards framework Aave introduced in V3.1, suggesting a convergent institutional DeFi governance pattern: on-chain majority voting for strategic parameters, elected committee fast-action for operational risk.

**Business model.** Ethena's revenue model is the basis trade: sUSDe deposits are backed by staked ETH (earning the Ethereum staking yield) and short perpetual futures positions on the same notional value (earning the funding rate when markets are in contango). The protocol retains a percentage of the aggregate yield as protocol revenue (interest margin), distributing the remainder to sUSDe holders as the annualised sUSDe yield. The basis trade yield is structurally dependent on crypto market conditions: when perpetual funding rates are positive (long-heavy markets, typical in bull cycles), sUSDe holders earn elevated yields; when funding rates turn negative (bear markets or short-heavy positioning), the yield compresses and can become negative, requiring Ethena's insurance fund to cover shortfalls.

**Competitive position.** Ethena competes in the on-chain stablecoin market against three classes of protocol: fiat-backed stablecoins (USDC, USDT), overcollateralised stablecoins (DAI/USDS from MakerDAO/Sky, crvUSD from Curve), and algorithmic stablecoins (FRAX, LUSD). Its differentiation is the native yield: unlike USDC, which offers no on-chain yield to holders, sUSDe provides a positive annual percentage yield (APY) derived from the basis trade, making it structurally attractive as a cash-equivalent in DeFi yield strategies. With $14.2 billion TVL, Ethena is one of the largest protocols in the dataset and the dominant synthetic dollar. Its risk is the "de-peg" scenario where a sustained negative funding environment exhausts the insurance fund — a structural weakness that the governance Risk Committee and insurance fund architecture are designed to contain. Multi-chain deployment (Ethereum mainnet with deployments on Arbitrum, Blast, and BNB Chain) positions sUSDe as a cross-chain yield-bearing stablecoin.

**Ecosystem illustration.** Ethena is the cleanest illustration of the liability-side TVL mechanism (§4.9.4, ANO-RWA-01). Its $14.2 billion TVL represents sUSDe depositor liabilities the protocol must service — not equity belonging to ENA tokenholders. The $1.8 billion ENA MCap prices the present value of fee capture on those liabilities, resolving the apparent 7.8× TVL/MCap anomaly: the protocol is correctly valued as a fee-extraction business operating at high leverage over its AUM. Ethena also confirms the RWA centralisation pattern (§4.6.4): the most commercially successful RWA-adjacent DApp is company-owned with hybrid governance, relying on Ethena Labs as the corporate entity managing custodial and regulatory obligations. The SEMI\_DECENTRALIZED coding reflects a protocol in transition rather than a stable decentralised endpoint.

## §4.16 Cross-Case Synthesis

The five case studies span the full governance spectrum from on-chain DAO (Uniswap, Aave) to hybrid committee-token governance (Ethena) to formal on-chain governance without operational decentralisation (Virtuals Protocol) to team-controlled tokenless operation (Polymarket). Read together, they yield four cross-cutting findings.

**Finding 1: Governance architecture is sector-contingent, not universally aspired to.** The three-layer triangulation reveals that the same governance structure that constitutes a competitive disadvantage in one sector (team control in DeFi DEX = vulnerability to credible fork) constitutes a competitive requirement in another (team control in prediction markets = speed of market creation and resolution authority). Cross-sectional metrics that score all DApps on the same governance ladder will systematically mischaracterise protocols whose operational requirements are incompatible with on-chain governance timelines. This reinforces the sector-disaggregated analytical approach adopted throughout Chapter 4.

**Finding 2: TVL, MCap, and user count each index a distinct economic logic.** Uniswap's governance-market-cap alignment (r=0.38 consistent with its position), Aave's interest-margin TVL leadership, Ethena's liability-side TVL/MCap inversion, Virtuals Protocol's MCap-per-user optionality premium, and Polymarket's volume-per-user outlier all demonstrate that no single metric reliably ranks protocol quality across business models. The anomaly register in §4.9 catalogues these divergences quantitatively; the case studies locate them in the specific operational logics that produce them.

**Finding 3: The governance token is not a reliable proxy for community control.** Uniswap and Aave demonstrate cases where governance tokens map credibly to binding on-chain authority. Virtuals Protocol demonstrates a case where on-chain governance machinery exists but operational control (AI model management) remains with the team. The anomaly documented in §4.9.1 — governance tokens coexisting with team-controlled governance — generalises to the AI sector through the structural constraint identified in INS-AI-01: the AI inference layer cannot currently be placed under on-chain governance, so the formal governance token architecture in AI-native DApps is structurally incomplete regardless of its design intent.

**Finding 4: Market concentration and governance quality co-vary but causation is ambiguous.** Uniswap and Aave occupy top-tier market-cap positions with fully on-chain governance; Polymarket commands a 95.3 per cent sector volume share with team-controlled governance and no token. The cross-sectional positive correlation (r=0.38) is real but heterogeneous across sectors. The correct interpretation is not that governance causes scale, nor that scale is independent of governance, but that within competitive sectors where credible neutrality is a differentiator — primarily DeFi — governance formalisation correlates with the institutional trust and composability that sustain protocol-level value capture. In winner-take-most sectors where speed and operational authority are the primary competitive variables — prediction markets, AI agent platforms — this correlation does not hold.

These four findings have direct implications for the discussion of research questions RQ1–RQ3 in Chapter 5, and for the evaluation frameworks proposed in §5.9. They are cited in the discussion where the interpretive implications of specific anomalies and sector-level findings are examined.
